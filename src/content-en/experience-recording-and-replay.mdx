*2026.11.02 · A thought about brain–computer interfaces*

![Traveling in Hulunbuir with high-school friends in summer 2025](/images/huolunbeier-memory.webp)

Last summer, after the college entrance examination, four high-school classmates and I traveled to Hulunbuir in Inner Mongolia without our parents.

It was a special experience. We saw the vast grasslands and lay together on a hill, watching the sunset and the rolling grassy slopes, almost empty of people. The wind moved through the grass as the sun slowly sank. There was a sense of freedom and ease that is difficult to put into words.

Recently, while organizing the photos and videos from that trip, I thought of those days again. An idea about a future application of brain–computer interfaces suddenly came to me:

**Could we one day record not only the scene around us, but what we actually felt at the time?**

Today's photographs and videos are, essentially, records of the outside environment. With VR, AI, and spatial computing, we can reconstruct a scene, simulating its light, sound, temperature, and surroundings in an attempt to bring memories back.

But I think there may be another possible route: directly recording a person's neural activity.

Suppose a future brain–computer interface could identify and record the neural activity associated with perception, emotion, and memory at a particular moment. Years later, could personalized neural stimulation reactivate activity associated with that experience, bringing someone closer to the subjective experience they had at the time?

It would be a movement from **Experience Recording** toward **Experience Replay**.

Compared with rebuilding an entire external environment, this approach might reach memory more directly and might reduce the substantial cost of simulating the environment. Of course, it would require solutions to complex problems of decoding, recording, storing, and restimulating neural activity. It remains a highly speculative, frontier idea.

If it became possible, its uses might extend beyond personal recollection. Perhaps we could preserve the experience of special moments, share an experience with others, or even explore applications in education, emotion research, and psychological intervention.

Of course, reproducing neural activity is not the same as truly returning to the past, nor does it mean we could completely duplicate what we felt then. Even so, I find the idea rich with possibilities.

**We have long worked to record what the world looks like. In the future, perhaps we could try to record how we experience it.**

Years later, opening that memory again might mean not only seeing Hulunbuir's sunset, but feeling the wind of that summer once more.
