![Uncertainty and reflection at the start of sophomore year](/images/sophomore-reflection.png)

I am a student in Nanjing University's Software Engineering Business Innovation program.

Since starting my second year, I have felt a growing sense of uncertainty. I do not know whether I am moving in the right direction, or where I should invest more of my time in the future. GPA, research, projects, competitions, scholarships—all of them seem to push me forward. Yet I often wonder: am I pursuing what I truly want, or simply following the path of an “excellent student” as others define it?

Throughout my first year, I lived with a contradiction.

When I first arrived at university, I continued living as I had in high school. I did not know what university life really meant, and I did not know how to relax. I had almost no leisure time. Each day revolved around studying, courses, and tasks; at most, I watched a few Bilibili videos after returning to my room on a weekend evening. I believed that the most important thing after entering university was to remain an outstanding student, so I spent a great deal of time in the library.

As time passed, however, I gradually found my place in university life. I learned to go out, explore the campus and the city, and accept that university should involve more than studying. For the first time, I felt that life should not just be an endless succession of completed tasks. It should also include experiences of my own.

Still, taken as a whole, I spent the vast majority of my time on studying and assorted responsibilities.

In the second semester of my first year, the courses began to wear me down. Compared with the first semester, my interest in my major's classes noticeably declined. Some software engineering courses, in particular, were difficult for me to find interesting. Many emphasized memorization and examination techniques instead of helping me experience software engineering's creativity. I grew increasingly tired of that way of learning.

By contrast, the economics, markets, and wealth-related parts of the business curriculum interested me more. I like studying how money flows, how companies create value, and how markets shape individual choices. But as an applicant from Zhejiang, I had not been able to choose a direction combining software engineering and economics. That leaves me with some regret about my choice of program.

Toward the end of the second semester, I gradually put more energy into activities beyond my courses. I started projects, participated in research, prepared for scholarship presentations, and joined different activities. In practice, I spent much less time on coursework, yet I still cared a great deal about my GPA.

That contradiction was difficult: I no longer wanted to devote all my time to examinations, but I could not entirely let go of the pressure attached to grades.

During the summer after my first year, I became intensely busy. I worked on many projects, joined many activities, and encountered many new directions. It was fulfilling, and I enjoyed the feeling of continually exploring and creating.

But I also noticed a problem in myself:

At the beginning of many things, I am full of enthusiasm. I enjoy imagining a project, learning something new, and building an idea from scratch. But when a project reaches its middle and later stages, requiring sustained effort and repeated attention to details, I often gradually lose interest. Many things remain at the “starting” stage without ever reaching the end.

I do not like that about myself.

I do not want to become someone who constantly looks for new opportunities but cannot settle down and build depth.

After starting my second year, the uncertainty became even clearer.

Over this past month or so, I have tried a kind of university life that I previously did not dare to have. I have begun having fun, going out to explore, and leaving time for rest. My first-year self, who had almost no leisure time, could hardly have imagined this.

At the same time, though, a new anxiety has emerged.

I want to study hard and remain competitive, but I find it difficult to take an interest in my current courses. Many no longer seem like classes where simply putting in enough time will secure a high grade. If I mechanically invest large amounts of time just for my GPA, I do not know whether that effort is worthwhile.

Yet putting time into independent learning, research, or projects could also become a sunk cost: I might invest a great deal of time without obtaining a clear result.

So now I am reconsidering a question:

What kind of person should I become in my second year?

Should I keep doing many projects and looking for more opportunities?

Or should I reduce the number of projects and put more energy into the foundations of my discipline?

Should I focus more on my GPA to maintain a so-called competitive advantage?

Or should I explore the things that truly interest me, such as economics, business, and creating with technology?

My greatest confusion is not that I do not know I should work hard. It is that I do not know which direction that effort should take.

Over the past year, I have been proving that I can do many things: maintain my grades, take part in research, complete projects, compete, and earn some recognition.

Now, however, I want to prove something else:

Can I find a direction that truly suits me and stay with it over the long term?

My second year may not be a year for endlessly expanding what I do. Perhaps it should be a time to put things in order again.

I need to learn to choose, rather than keep adding tasks;

to persevere, rather than keep starting;

and to find something I truly want to invest in over the long term, rather than simply chase short-term results.

I do not have the answers yet.

But at least I am beginning to see that the truly difficult part of university is not how to work hard. It is deciding why I am working hard.
